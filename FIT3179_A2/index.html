Australian nut industry | 2024–25

FROM AUSTRALIAN ORCHARDS TO THE WORLD

Seven crops. Three kinds of concentration. One uneven industry.

A handful of states grow most of Australia's nuts. One crop produces most of their value. And a few overseas buyers dominate several export markets. Follow the evidence from orchard to export destination to see why a strong industry total can hide very different stories.

Source: Hort Innovation, Australian Horticulture Statistics Handbook 2024–25. World boundaries: Natural Earth / FIT3179 teaching resources. Source details

Data & methodology

The map combines the latest available country-level destination breakdown for each nut type. Almond and Macadamia use 2024 data because their 2025 country-level breakdowns are not fully reported in the source; other nuts use 2025 data.

The all-nuts view aggregates reported export tonnes across different nut types and measurement bases (mostly kernel weight equivalent, meaning shell-free weight; Walnut and Pistachio use a different or unstated basis, together under 6% of the total shown). It should therefore be read as an approximate indication of the geographic distribution of reported exports, not an exact, standardised national export total.

Export volume is represented using a square-root line-width scale to keep smaller flows visible. Destination points represent approximate country locations; lines illustrate connections between Australia and destination countries, not shipping routes, transport modes or ports.

The "Other" category (14.6% of reported export tonnage) is excluded because no specific country is identified in the source; percentages therefore refer only to named destinations. Destinations reported as "<1 tonne" are displayed with a minimum visible line width — their exact quantity is unknown and is not assumed to equal one tonne.

The other side of trade

An exporter can also be a net importer.

Comparing export and import values reveals a different picture for several smaller crops.

Chart 10 / Export–import comparison

Almond earns a $1.3bn surplus; walnut, pistachio and hazelnut import more than they export

Reported export and import values in the handbook, 2025. All seven crops are included; a connector is drawn only where both values are reported.

Key takeaway

Exporting a crop does not imply a trade surplus.

Walnut, pistachio and hazelnut each have higher reported import values than export values. Almond has a $1,321.0m surplus.

Reading the chart

Teal circles show exports; brown diamonds show imports. A connecting line shows the monetary gap. The default view excludes almond to reveal smaller gaps. Check the box to include it. Missing endpoints are identified in each row, never positioned at zero.

Source: Hort Innovation, Australian Horticulture Statistics Handbook 2024–25. Source details

Data & methodology

Balance = reported export value − reported import value, in nominal A$ million. It is a monetary trade balance, not a physical production or consumption share.

Macadamia and pecan remain visible with reported exports of $246.6m and $2.5m. Their import values are printed as “–”; chestnut’s export value is “–” and its import value is reported as <$0.1m. The bounded import value is shown in text rather than plotted as an exact point. Here, “–” means no numeric value reported, not a confirmed zero. Pecan’s supply identity is consistent with zero import tonnage, but does not independently establish a published monetary import value. No balance is calculated for these incomplete pairs.

Three things to take away

Concentrated at home

Uneven growth

Data & methodology

Production is measured on a kernel weight equivalent (shell-free) basis for almond, pecan, hazelnut and chestnut, and an in-shell basis for macadamia, walnut and pistachio. Trade and supply may use different bases: macadamia uses kernel weight equivalent; walnut and the industry aggregate mix kernel and in-shell quantities; pistachio’s trade/supply basis is unspecified. Monetary comparisons use Australian dollars; physical quantities retain their reported bases.

Each chart uses the most recent year the handbook reports for that nut; where a 2025 breakdown was not yet published (e.g. Almond and Macadamia's export destinations, which use 2024 figures), the chart note beneath it says so.

A small number of figures in the source handbook do not fully reconcile across tables (e.g. Pecan state totals, the All Nuts production-value sum); these are disclosed individually beneath the relevant chart rather than silently adjusted. Destinations reported as "<1 tonne" are kept as a real, unknown positive quantity below the table's rounding threshold — never treated as exactly 1.
